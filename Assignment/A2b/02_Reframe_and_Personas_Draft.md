## Part A — Persona data sheet 解读：要不要拆成两个 persona？

看你的 data sheet，三个颜色（对应 3 位受访者）在多条变量轴上不是随机分布的，而是稳定地分成两群：

**群体 1 —— 绿色 + 粉色，几乎每条轴都黏在一起**
- Male
- Give up and live with the issue
- Waits it out until the contract ends
- Continuous condition（把问题看成持续状态，不是一次性事件）
- Repair issue hard to measure

**群体 2 —— 橙色，单独站在另一端**
- Female
- Stick to raise the issue（反复去问/去追）
- Expects it to be fixed / One-off breakdown（把问题当成"一次性事件，该被解决"）
- 但同时落在 "get ambiguous response" 一侧——追问了，回应还是不清不楚

这正是老师说的"两极分化"：不是同一个变量偶然分开，是**engagement stance**（放弃 vs. 坚持追问）和 **expectation framing**（当成持续背景状态 vs. 当成该被解决的一次性事件）这两条主轴上，三个人稳定地分成两群。符合 Week6 lecture 的方法："look for people that cluster together across multiple variables"。

**建议：拆成两个 persona**，而不是硬凑成一个"平均值"人物（Week6 lecture 原话："personas are not... an average of all the people you have interviewed"）：

- **Persona A（绿+粉群体）——暂定"The Quiet Ender"**：BC 应该就属于这一群（男性、student、backstory 里提到"国际学生、多国生活经验"——符合"把问题当背景噪音、等 contract 到期"这种倾向，短期租约的人没有动力去打持久战）
- **Persona B（橙色）——暂定"The Persistent Asker"**：主动反复追问，但依然拿不到清楚答案

这个拆分直接支撑你们的 reframed brief：A 的沉默和 B 的追问看起来相反，但**根源是同一件事**——没有可见的状态、没有明确的负责人，只是两种人对同一个缺口的不同反应。这比只做一个"平均 persona"更有说服力，也更贴题（brief 要求至少两个 stakeholder 群体，两个 persona 刚好能撑住这一条）。

**需要你确认（我不能替你编事实）：**
1. 橙色是哪位受访者（学生公寓 / 社会住房 / access needs 里的哪一个）？我的猜测是不是学生的 BC 是绿或粉之一——但绿粉具体对应哪两位、橙色对应哪位，需要你对照原始访谈确认。
2. Persona B（橙色）的 profile 字段（name/occupation/age/gender）需要你从对应受访者的转录里取——不能瞎编具体人口统计信息。

## Part D — Reframed Design Brief（按你的模板字段填）

**Background**
> Across our ~12 interviews (student accommodation, social housing, and residents with access needs), residents described the same structural gap: the law requires shared-facility repairs (*Strata Schemes Management Act 2015* (NSW) s106; *Residential Tenancies Act 2010* (NSW) s63) but sets no timeframe. Our affinity diagram of these interviews surfaced seven recurring pain points — unclear standards for how issues are handled, status that's never updated, replies that are unhelpful or absent, unclear responsible parties, waits longer than expected, repeated status-checking, and a wait that disrupts residents' other life plans. Reported cases mirror this: a social-housing hot water outage took 9 days until a minister's office intervened and resolved it in 2 hours (Gerathy, 2024); a wheelchair user was left stranded overnight by an unexplained lift outage (Coghill, 2025).

**The problem/opportunity**
> How might we make the status, timeline and responsible party of a reported issue visible, for residents who currently have no way to know where their case stands or who is acting on it?

**Aim**
> To reduce the uncertainty residents experience while waiting for a shared-facility repair, by making the existing repair process visible — without increasing repair capacity or promising faster fixes — so residents can plan around a known next update instead of an unknown end date.

**Deliverable/s**
> From our affinity diagrams, two directions surfaced consistently across both engagement patterns (the "quiet enders" and the "persistent askers"): (1) a visible status point residents can check without having to ask anyone, and (2) a guaranteed, defined communication moment after a fault is reported. We are developing these as a low-fidelity Visible Repair Queue Board and a Repair Receipt Card.

## Part E — Problem/Opportunity Space（WHO / WHAT / WHEN / WHAT resulting in → 合并段落）

- **WHO is impacted?** Residents in shared apartment buildings — across student accommodation, social housing, and residents with access needs — who report a shared-facility fault (lift, hot water, door).
- **WHAT is the problem/opportunity?** They can report a fault, but cannot see its status, who's responsible for it, or when they'll next hear anything — reporting is possible, tracking or influencing what happens next is not.
- **WHEN does it happen?** From the moment a fault is reported until it's resolved — an undefined period with no legislated timeframe, during which residents repeatedly check for updates and get none.
- **WHAT is it resulting in?** Residents either disengage — give up raising it and wait it out as background condition — or over-escalate — keep chasing a response that stays ambiguous. Either way, the uncertainty disrupts their ability to plan the rest of their life around it, and it lands hardest on residents for whom the outage removes access, not just convenience.

**Our problem/opportunity space is:**
> Residents in shared apartment buildings — across student accommodation, social housing, and residents with access needs — who report a shared-facility fault but cannot see its status, who is responsible, or when they'll next hear anything, for the entire undefined period between reporting and resolution. Left without that visibility, residents either disengage and wait it out, or repeatedly chase a response that stays ambiguous — and either way, the uncertainty disrupts their ability to plan the rest of their lives around it.
